# Ximenean conventions and the judge's rulebook

The standard for fairness in the English cryptic tradition (Ximenes, D.S.
Macnee's *Ximenes on the Art of the Crossword*, upheld by Azed). A clue may be
witty, misleading and hard, but it must be *fair*: the cryptic reading must
lead to the answer by legitimate operations, and nothing may be left
unindicated. The surface reading is allowed to lie; the cryptic reading is not.

## The three tests a sound clue passes

1. **A single, accurate definition.** Exactly one part of the clue defines the
   answer, placed at the start or the end (never buried in the middle). The
   definition must match the answer in part of speech and in number/tense. A
   plural answer needs a plural definition; a past-tense answer, a past-tense
   definition.

2. **Fair, complete wordplay.** A second, independent set of instructions that
   also yields the answer. Every letter of the answer must be accounted for by
   the wordplay, and every operation (anagram, reversal, deletion, container,
   selection, homophone) must be signalled by a legitimate indicator. Nothing
   is added or removed silently.

3. **Cryptic grammar.** Read as instructions, the wordplay must parse. An
   anagram indicator must actually touch its fodder; a container indicator must
   sit between the two things; a reversal indicator must apply to something
   reversible. "The cryptic grammar and the surface grammar are two different
   sentences occupying the same words."

## What Ximenean rules forbid

- **Indirect anagrams**: anagramming a synonym the solver must first guess. The
  anagram fodder must appear as explicit letters in the clue.
- **Unindicated operations**: no silent reversals, deletions or insertions.
- **Definition by example without a marker**: "flower" for ROSE needs "say",
  "perhaps", "for one". Otherwise it claims rose *is* flower generically.
- **Two definitions masquerading as wordplay** unless it is a genuine
  double-definition clue (which has no other wordplay and no indicators).
- **Redundant words**: every word does a job (definition, fodder, indicator or
  fair link). Padding to smooth the surface is a fault, however tempting.
- **Wrong-length or wrong-sense definitions**, and homophones that only work in
  one accent without acknowledgement.

## Clue types (the wordplay families)

- **Anagram**: fodder + anagram indicator. `NOTED` → "Toned oddly".
- **Charade**: pieces written in sequence. `CARPET` → CAR + PET.
- **Container/contents**: one string inside another. `A` in `BET` → BAET…
- **Hidden**: answer sits unbroken inside the fodder, with a hidden indicator.
- **Reversal**: a string reversed, with a reversal indicator (mind across/down).
- **Deletion**: letters removed from a longer word, indicated.
- **Homophone**: a word that sounds like the answer (or a part), indicated.
- **Double definition**: two distinct definitions, no other wordplay, no
  indicators. Best when the two senses are far apart and one is a surprise.
- **Cryptic definition**: one witty, misleading definition, no separate
  wordplay, often ending in "?". **Disallowed in this house style** because it
  carries no wordplay (see the wordplay rule below). Keep the wit for a
  misdirecting definition inside a proper wordplay clue.
- **&lit ("all-in-one")**: the entire clue is both definition and wordplay.
  Marked with "!". The prize form when it comes off.
- **Composite**: several operations combined (e.g. anagram of one part next to
  a reversal of another). Powerful, but keep it parseable.

## Surface: what "smooth" means here

A smooth surface reads as a natural, self-contained sentence or phrase whose
plain meaning has nothing to do with its cryptic function. The reader should
not be able to tell which words are definition, fodder, indicator or link. The
best surfaces achieve this with:

- **A misdirecting definition**: a word whose obvious sense in the surface is
  not the sense that defines the answer. A verb read as a noun, a proper noun
  read as a common one, "flower" read as a bloom but meaning a river. This is
  the single biggest source of satisfaction and is fully Ximenean.
- **Indicators that earn their place in the surface**: "cooked" as an anagram
  indicator inside a sentence about dinner, not a bare "anag. of".
- **No tell-tale seams**: avoid stacking indicators, avoid link words that only
  make cryptic sense, avoid fodder that reads as a random letter-string.

## The wordplay rule (hard gate)

Every clue must contain wordplay: anagram, charade, container, hidden, reversal,
deletion, homophone, or any combination, or an &lit whose whole surface reads as
wordplay. The **only** wordplay-free form allowed is the double definition (two
distinct definitions). A bare cryptic definition, a single witty definition with
no letter-level wordplay, fails outright no matter how good the surface. The wit
of a cryptic definition should instead be carried by a misdirecting definition
sitting inside a proper wordplay clue.

## The judge's scoring (0–5 each, plus notes)

- **Wordplay present** (hard gate): does the clue carry wordplay, or is it a
  double definition? A bare cryptic definition fails here regardless of wit.
- **Soundness** (hard gate): does it pass the three tests? Any unindicated
  operation, indirect anagram, wrong-length or misplaced definition caps the
  clue low regardless of wit. State the full parse to prove it.
- **Surface**: does it read as a natural, plausible phrase? Any awkwardness,
  padding or non-idiomatic join costs marks.
- **Misdirection / definition wit**: how well hidden are the seams; is the
  definition a satisfying pun or part-of-speech shift.
- **Economy**: no wasted words; indicator and fodder pull double duty.
- **Difficulty**: pitched to give the solver a fair fight and a fair "aha".

Report each candidate with: the clue, the enumeration, the definition
(underlined or marked), the wordplay parse with letter accounting, the
indicator(s) identified, and a one-line note on the surface's misdirection.
